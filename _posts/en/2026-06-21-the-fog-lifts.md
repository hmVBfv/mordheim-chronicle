Nineteen days lay between the night at the quay and the night this account concerns. In that time the frost went out of the cobbles, the Stir rose two hands' breadth, and the fog that had stood over Quayside like wet wool tore open and gave the city back. Anyone who took that to mean there was more to be seen had not yet spent long enough in Mordheim.

Roberta van Dorp did not bury Sir Dieter von Wurtzen. Nobody in this city is buried who has not paid for it in advance; you burn what you can gather up and you write down the rest. So she did what a trader does. She opened her book and entered what the quay had cost, and because a knight and three barrels of pitch stood in the same column, the two of them looked like items of the same kind. Then she took the wagon east, away from the water. The quay had shown her what she already knew: where three bands collide in a fog, nobody earns anything. In the merchants' quarter, by contrast, there had once been people who kept books as she did, and books survive a good deal that people do not. At the gate she hired whoever was standing there. She did not ask for names. She asked for hands, and whether a man had ever brought a wagon down an alley in the dark.

Among the Reavers the counting went differently. Rangvald had held the field and by the third day no longer knew what for. There was no gold on that quay, there were no people on it who could be made to kneel, and a victory nobody watched is hardly a victory to a Norseman. Styrkarr Skysplitter saw it otherwise, and said so: the stone they had prised out of the rubble glowed green when you held it in the dark, and it had come down out of the sky. What comes out of the sky does not fall — it is thrown, and whoever throws it means something by it. And what is meant, the seer said, stands written down among the people who write everything down, because otherwise they forget it. That was the point at which the seer asked his chieftain for the one thing he did not care to give — time. For nine days the Reavers did not go through the northern quarters burning and taking, but put a single question to everyone they caught: where was it written down, what the comet is. Two died under the asking without ever knowing what had been asked. The third named the Great Library, and died as well, only later.

That not one man among the Reavers could read was, to Styrkarr, no objection. He did not intend to read. He intended to hold something in his hands that an Empire man had thought worth setting down, and then to see what Tchar made of it.

The Children of Sotek carried their dead away. There were two of them, a skink and a saurus, and they carried them down until there was water over them again, which took a night and half a day and served nobody but themselves. Yingling said nothing in that time that a stranger would have understood. What he was thinking can be read off what he did afterwards. He had drawn up an account at the quay, and it would not come out even: the northmen had not been where the plunder lay. They had been where the water comes out of the cellars. A man hunting gold goes to the houses. Anyone who follows the drains is after something else.

So the Children did not follow the library. They followed the northmen — through pipes that have stood half full of silt since the comet, under streets along which men in furs and axes walked above them, always two days behind and never closer. Wonton stayed back. His leg had not forgotten the winter, and the drains are cold.

Three roads came to the same hall that way, and not one of them led there because there was anything in it worth having. The caravan was looking for a detour, the Reavers for an answer, and the Children for the Reavers. Only the roof was already open before any of them arrived.
