### The Tower of Burned Pages

There are places in Mordheim where the comet shattered not only stone but memory itself. The Great Library had once been the memory of the city — a hall of such height that its ceiling was lost in darkness, and shelves that rose like stone cliffs into that very darkness, filled with scrolls and folios from a thousand years of trade, war and faith. Now all of that was gone. Of the roof nothing remained but the charred stumps of its beams along the tops of the walls; the whole building lay open to the sky, and the pale light of early spring fell all the way to the floor, wan and without warmth. The great shelves had toppled like felled trees, one upon another — only along the outer walls did a few still stand, wedged between stone and buttress, holding rows of spines no one would ever open again. What had once been knowledge lay in drifts of ash and shattered parchment across the floor, black and light as snow, swirling up at every step and dancing in the shafts of light. The marble beneath the soot was cracked and warped; at the window arches the lead of the glazing hung in frozen drops, where the heat had set it flowing, and over everything lay the smell of cold smoke and wet stone. Where scholars had once whispered, now only the wind whispered through the ruins.

A cobbled square lay before the building, and a broad flight of steps rose across it to the portal. A stone owl crouched at either side of the top step, each the size of a child, head turned toward the street. One had survived the fall of the pediment; the other had lost a wing. The pediment itself lay in the middle of the square where it had come down, face up: a set of scales cut in stone, both pans level, and around them signs that few had been able to read even before the comet. Beside it a toppled column in three pieces, each as long as a wagon. At the edge of the square, half buried in rubble, stood the round rim of a well, and the shaft beneath it went deeper than a well needs to go.

Rangvald's Reavers came from the north of the city, plundering, burning, their trail as plain as a fire in the night. When Rangvald and Styrkarr Skysplitter stood before the collapsed archways of the library, they saw not merely ruins — they saw a promise. Somewhere beneath this mountain of burned knowledge, they believed, must lie what they sought: the nature of the wyrdstone, the true meaning of the comet, perhaps even lore that counted as forbidden even among the servants of Chaos. They entered, never guessing that they drew two shadows with them into the dark.

For they had been followed. Through the sewers, through the damp dark beneath the city, the Children of Sotek had tracked every trace of blood and smoke the Chaos warriors left behind. What drove them was no hunting-lust such as a beast knows — it was older, colder, more implacable. Long before Mordheim had a name, long before men even learned to set stone upon stone, it was the powers of Chaos that broke through the shattered portals at the poles of the world and laid the great work of the Old Ones in ruins. Ever since, no Skink, no Saurus, no Slann has known a purpose that sits deeper than this: to hunt Chaos wherever it shows itself, and to extinguish it, without hesitation, without mercy. For the Lizardmen, who rose up through that very well at the edge of the square and neared the great stair in silence, the stench of blood was no coincidence. It was a summons. And they had come to answer it.

A third trail led to the library that day — one no one had planned. The Ardent Caravan had in fact been on its way to the Merchants' Quarter, but the fight at Quayside had blocked its direct path and forced it to a detour. Roberta van Dorp — Robba, as her people called her — led her caravan eastward through silent alleys, past collapsed houses and burned-out chapels in which spring already drove its first pale green through the cracks. Whether it was chance or something merely disguised as chance she would never learn: the road led her to the very place where Rangvald's Reavers and the Children of Sotek were already meeting. In hope of scrolls, rare volumes, or even a scrap of spared magic among the rubble, the caravan too entered the library — with no notion of what it was walking into.

### Three Powers Amid the Shelves

The war-cries reached the caravan before it could even get its bearings, echoing off the burst walls and thrown back many times over, until no one could say from which direction they came. Through holes in the shattered outer walls, Skinks had already squeezed, supple as water through cracks in stone, and had brought Ulfrik the Impaler to bay in the twilight of the ruins. The fight flared up, without warning, without mercy.

On the other side of the building, where the caravan itself had entered, Saurus warriors and further Skinks — at their head the Totem Warrior **Blutvater Ootl** — pressed forward through the burst halls, a flood-tide of scales and fury that whirled up the drifts of ash until the air was black with dancing flakes of parchment. But the Chaos hounds waited already in the dark, and with a cry that was more incantation than command, Packmaster Styrkarr Skysplitter turned to Tchar, the Great Eagle — in the runes of his tribe the wise eagle — and levelled his accusation at the oncoming ranks. Again he chose the untried, the young, those with nothing to show but their eagerness, and again the Great Eagle's verdict broke over them, heavy in exact measure with how empty of deeds their lives had been.

Ulfrik fought as he always fought: dogged, unyielding, giving not a step — until sheer numbers at last forced him to his knees and he went down. The Chaos hounds, whipped on by their Packmaster's fury, hurled themselves with foaming jaws into the ranks of the Lizardmen, tearing wounds that would burn long after this day — until they too, spent, bleeding, drained by their own savagery, collapsed and could carry the fight no further. Amid the tumult Rangvald himself swung round a burst door and struck down a Lizardman, while a lone Skink seized the moment of confusion that had settled over the field: he rushed at the Packmaster, beneath his guard, beneath his very gaze, and wrestled the dark seer to the ground with bare claws. It was a creature with no name and no deeds to it, one of the very sort Styrkarr had spent the day passing judgement upon — and no accusation in the world slowed it down.

Far from this melee, high above the burning shelves, a Chaos warrior had posted himself on the ruined first floor of the library, where a half-collapsed gallery still jutted out over the hall — a hawk looking down upon his field. It was the Tilean crossbowman Marcus who first marked him. Together with Magister Julius Broun — who hid his forbidden art from the witch hunters beneath Robba's wagon-covers — he brought bolt and magic-steeped arrow to bear, and within a breath the air sang. The Chaos warrior staggered, fell, struck hard upon the burst stone floor — yet he stayed conscious, a predator that had merely stumbled.

Below, in the heart of the library, Sir Honnung von Hoiser drove his blade into a charge, at his side Werner and Walter, Ottilie, Eckhardt and Eberhardt — six against three waiting Chaos warriors — among them the marauders **Defiler** and **Skullsmasher** — who had set themselves among the toppled shelves like wolves in a trap. As their blades clashed and sparks flew through the dim half-light, Robba herself withdrew quietly into one of the rear reading-rooms, leaving the din of battle behind her, where the shelves still half stood and the dust hung undisturbed in the shafts of light, in search of that one find that would make the whole perilous journey worth it.

But the fight below in the hall turned. Several of the mercenaries who had charged with Sir Honnung went down beneath the blades of the waiting Chaos warriors, one after another torn from the fray. And then, without warning, without anyone having glanced upward: the Chaos warrior from the first floor — the hawk who had staggered but a moment before — rose again, stepped to the edge of the burst gallery, and flung himself in a daring leap through the depths straight down upon Ottilie. She had no chance to evade him. The impact struck her to the ground once more, out of action, a second time in this young campaign.

### Retreat into the Dark

So the fight ended as it had begun: in stalemate, in blood, without a true victor. The Lizardmen, their ranks broken, withdrew into the sewers from which they had come — their advance shattered by the blades of the Chaos warriors and the sacrifice of countless Chaos hounds, and even the ancient duty that had driven them here now demanded patience rather than more blood.

The Ardent Caravan too seized the moment to withdraw. They had not come to fall to the last man against lizards or Chaos warriors from the north — they were merchants, not warriors by trade, and their aim was to find rare things and sell them at a profit, not to spill their blood for ruins that belonged to no one any longer. And even Rangvald at last ordered the retreat. His band, hard as they were, had first to recover from this unexpected, costly engagement before they could move on.

In the Great Library the old familiar silence returned — that particular silence known only to ruins that have seen too much. The dust settled slowly once more, the pale spring light wandered across the burst marble, and only the blood on the stones, the shattered shelves and the freshly churned ash still told that here, for one brief, bitter moment, three powers had met that none of them had planned.

### Aftermath

The fight for the library exacted its toll off the field as well. The Skink's claws had opened **Styrkarr Skysplitter**'s right forearm down to the sinew — and that arm, of all of them, is the instrument of his office. A seer of Tchar does not call with his voice alone; he draws what he accuses upon the air with an outstretched hand, and where the fingers will not follow, neither does the verdict. For weeks the arm lay splinted in furs, swollen and hot, and when Styrkarr tried to raise it, it shook like an old man's. His men did not say it aloud, but every one of them thought it: that a nameless creature had brought down one marked by the Great Eagle and taken him out of the field for a month. When the Reavers next set out, the Packmaster will stay behind in camp, and they will march without the voice of their god.

**Sir Honnung von Hoiser**, for his part, proved that not every rashness happens in combat. A man who no longer knows fear no longer weighs the odds either, and so after the battle he lowered himself into one of the library's well-shafts, convinced he had seen a green glimmer down in the dark. The stone was slick with the soot of years; he lost his grip, struck the water and went under. What he swallowed was the water the comet has poisoned — it tastes of metal and burns in the throat for days afterwards. They hauled him out coughing and empty-handed. The fever that followed put the man whom nothing on the battlefield could stop flat on his back for weeks: he will not ride out to the next battle, and Robba is said to have remarked that this is exactly the kind of loss for which there is nobody to blame.

Yet not all that came out of that day was loss. In Tilea, where **Marcus** learned his trade, they drill crossbowmen not for speed but for composure: kneel behind the pavise, wind, breathe, and let the bolt go only when it is already true — and do it in the middle of a battlefield that is screaming a man to pieces. He had practised it all his life and never once found out whether it would hold. In the library he found out. While friends went down below him, while ash and burning parchment turned the air black and no one could say which direction the next attack would come from, he knelt among the toppled shelves, counted off the beat of his own heart, and waited until the hawk on the gallery stood still. The bolt struck; the Chaos warrior fell. It was not the hit that mattered — Marcus had made plenty of hits — but that in all that din he had got to the shot at all.

His comrades noticed. Mercenaries do not measure one another by virtue but by who still does what he is paid for at the worst possible moment, and by that reckoning Marcus stood higher that day than most. After the library it could no longer be overlooked: in the eyes of the caravan the sell-sword Marcus had become a man with a name. They no longer place him where numbers are wanted, but where everything turns on a single steady shot.
