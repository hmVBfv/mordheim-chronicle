### The Opening Bouts

The Pitmaster had a decision to make that evening and nobody gave him time to make it. There were lizards standing in his menagerie who had come up out of the water and had stopped one of his stable hands from ever speaking again, and a man in that position has two choices: he can try to throw them out, or he can put them on the card. Nobody who earns his living from people wanting to see something new throws anything out. He had it announced that the house had engaged fighters out of Lustria for the night, which was a lie, and the tiers took it from him because they wanted to believe it. Wendel Krumm wrote odds for it in the same minute and could not have explained them to himself.

There is a moment, just before the first bout is called, when everything is still possible. The crowd has settled, the candles stand steady, the boy with the rake finishes and walks out backwards so that he takes his own footprints with him. From one side the air smelled of wet hide and river, because that was where the Children of Sotek stood with the water still in their scales. From the other, of tallow and beer and oil. And over all of it lay the warm, floury smell of old grain that the walls have not shed in thirty years, so friendly that in that room it turns into something obscene.

Wendel Krumm called the odds three times over, a little louder each time, in the voice of a man who believes his own numbers. The same three blazons now stood four times beneath one another in his ledger, at four places with nothing to do with one another. He said nothing about it. Wagering lives on people believing in chance, and a bookmaker who announces out loud that something here is running to a plan takes no more bets that evening.

The sand was freshly raked when they walked out onto it, in long straight lines, and only around the pillar had the boy with the rake gone round in a curve. The pillar stands a little off centre, a remnant of the old vaulting with iron rings at chest height, two of them still carrying their chains.

Around its foot lies the spine, and at the near end of it, half sunk in the sand and tipped onto its side, the skull. It is as long as a man is from hip to crown, wedge-shaped, the jaw part open. There are more holes in that jaw than teeth. Anyone wanting to know how long the Pitmaster has been earning off this animal need only count what is missing. It belongs to the pit the way the barrier and the candles do, and the regulars point it out to newcomers so the newcomers have something to shake their heads at.

The Pitmaster began the way he begins every evening: three single bouts, so that the crowd could get to know the strangers before betting on them. Against each of the three he put one of his light men — barefoot fellows in nothing but a helmet, a bundle of javelins in the crook of one arm and a dagger at the belt, who shed anything that might slow them down. He keeps them for the openers because they rarely die and the crowd hates them, and because they are more dangerous in this bowl than they would be anywhere else: a man who fights every night in the same hollow knows where the sand holds, where the barrier sits at your back, and how far a man will give ground before he feels the iron spikes. Which of their own each warband sent out was left to the warbands. A pitmaster does not make his living from his people winning; he makes it from the spectators believing they might lose.

Who goes into the sand for the Reavers is Rangvald's decision, but the seer is asked first. It has been that way for years. The chieftain is the strongest man in the warband and nobody would argue otherwise, but the signs are read by Styrkarr Skysplitter, and whoever reads the signs says on which days strength is worth anything. Styrkarr looked at the sand for a while, then at the tiers, then said a single sentence, and Rangvald nodded.

Rangvald sent Ulfrik the Impaler, and the tiers belonged to him before he had reached the middle. A Norseman stripped to the waist with a reputation that arrives ahead of him is exactly what people pay for. Across from him stood a wiry, barefoot man in a battered helmet with a dagger and nothing else, having left his javelins behind altogether, who did nothing worth describing except not be where Ulfrik's blows landed. Twice the Impaler's blade went through air that had contained somebody a moment earlier, and both times without the slightest hurry — the man slipped aside like someone who has done it for coppers for years and never breaks a sweat at it. The third time he was inside, where a long weapon is no use, and he went to work with the dagger the way men work with daggers in pits: short, deep and without ceremony. The crowd roared, but it was no longer roaring for the Norseman. Ulfrik did not leave the sand on his own legs.

Second, the caravan sent Julius Broun, and Robba had a reason she considered a good one: a man who can end a fight from ten paces need not walk into it at all. The Magister went out like someone entering a room where he does not dare touch anything, and there was a reason for that too. Men in heavy coats were sitting in the front row and betting loudly, and a single raised word would have ended his evening more finally than any opponent could.

He was not even given the choice. His opponent came off the mark the way those men come off the mark, and out of the run itself, before Broun had so much as measured the distance, the javelin left his hand. It struck early and it struck true. The Magister lay in the sand without having raised his hand at all — which, strictly speaking, was the only way he was going to survive that bout. On the far side of the oval Ulfrik watched it from the ground and Rangvald watched it standing, and neither of them was satisfied. They had come to teach the wizard a lesson. The house had taken it away from them and converted it into copper.

The third bout went to Broodfather Qotl, and it went to him so quickly that Krumm claimed afterwards he had not been able to change the odds in time. Since the estate the saurus had done almost nothing the others would have read as movement of any kind. Now he stood in the sand, let his opponent come, let him strike twice and struck back once. Sidestepping helped nobody here; you can slip the first blow, not the only one. It was not a fight. It was a statement of fact. The crowd went quiet for a moment and then began to stamp, and the brick dust came down out of the ceiling joints onto the shoulders of everyone seated lower.

Krumm struck out his figures and wrote new ones, and the crowd that had backed the Norseman an hour before now backed the lizards. That is how it goes down there: a reputation a man spends a year building above ground lasts about two minutes in the sand.

Robba van Dorp watched from the barrier and did her arithmetic, because she always does. Two of her people were down before the evening had properly started, and the share the Pitmaster pays losers does not repair a wagon. Ottilie stood beside her, who had no business in the sand and was down there all the same, because a merchant trading on credit has to leave a pledge and Robba had nothing else the Pitmaster would have valued. The girl said nothing. She looked at the tiers, at the stalls, at the lamps with the green light in them, and it was only later that Robba realised she had been committing all of it to memory.

### The Sand Fills

After that the Pitmaster had all four doors hauled open at once, and whatever was of any use in the three warbands walked out. Ulfrik was back, roughly strapped together by a man who is paid for it and asks no questions, and Julius Broun came out last, pale and without the javelin but on his own feet. The house sends back whatever can still stand. That is what people come for.

It did not take long before the Pitmaster added to the evening. Where the barrier is lowest, three stalls were hauled open, and what came out of them was grey and long and far too fast for its size.

Two of the three warbands knew these animals. After the night in the Rich Quarter, when the jeweller's pack ran masterless through the streets, the Pitmaster's men had combed the district and taken with nets and meat whatever was left. They are kept hungry, because a fed wolf lies down, and these had brought nothing down since that night. Sir Honnung recognised them by the way they ran and said something that was lost in the noise.

The first did not get far. Broodfather Qotl, who had been standing in an opening bout a quarter of an hour earlier, saw it coming and did nothing a man would have done: he stood still until it was too late to move, and took the animal in mid-leap. It was dead before it touched the sand again, and the tiers, who had bet on three rounds of chasing, whistled.

The second ran into the Norsemen, and specifically into Wulfrik Shiptaker, who learned his trade at sea and to whom an animal coming out of a corner is nothing new. He knocked it down without breaking stride and left it there, because the next opponent was already between him and the wolf. Rangvald found the animal a round later and finished it without hurrying. A chieftain does not strike quickly; he strikes when he connects, and he does it where the tiers can see.

The third wolf decided the evening for the caravan, though not in the way Robba had pictured. It went for Sir Honnung von Hoiser, and the knight from Reikland took it the way he takes everything: loudly and without giving ground. This time that was not enough. A rapier is a weapon made for men — it holds them off and finds the gap between two ribs, and the dagger in the left hand is there to catch whatever gets past the point. A wolf does not come at range. It comes low and fast and all in one piece, and the animal was under the blade before Honnung could bring it down. The knight went over backwards with a hundred pounds of wolf on top of him and his own armour adding to the weight.

What happened next will be told among the three warbands for a while yet, and depending on who is telling it, it ends differently. On the opposite side of the sand stood Wonton, who saw a wolf half draped over a man on the ground and drew his short bow. A skink with prey in front of him does not stop to consider whose chest it happens to be sitting on. The arrow did not go into the wolf. It went into Sir Honnung, glanced off what the knight had spent his money on, and came to nothing — except in the one respect that Marcus had seen it. The Tilean swung his crossbow round without a word and shot back across the full width of the sand, and Wonton was suddenly sitting where he had been standing. He was up again on his next turn, but the matter between the two marksmen is not closed.

The wolf itself was finished by what came from the side nobody was watching. Ottilie never learned to fight. She learned to carry, to count, and to keep her mouth shut at the right moment, and once, in the fog at the quayside, she did something she has not talked about since.

What she did learn she has carried under her coat since Marienburg: a pistol, bought from a woman who escorts trade caravans and knows what happens to people on the road. It has one shot in it. After that it is a handful of iron you cannot hit anyone with. Ottilie walked three paces closer, because a shot from three paces hits and a shot from ten does not, waited until the animal wrenched its head round, and pulled the trigger.

In a room where the smoke has nowhere to go, a pistol shot is something you feel in the chest. The tiers, which had been roaring all evening, went completely silent for the space of a heartbeat. Sir Honnung shoved the carcass off his chest, got up, and understood that he was no longer fighting. Up in the tiers a man stood and called out odds nobody had taken, because nobody had bet on the girl.

### The House Steps In

The Pitmaster had the horn blown when the last wolf went down. The crowd knew the note and was already on its feet before the gates opened.

And with them the warbands' own gates opened. Whatever had been waiting in the stalls — sell-swords, marauders, skinks — came out in the same moment, and the sand, which a minute earlier had held thirty men comfortably, no longer did.

They did not charge; they came out in order, and that was what tightened three sets of throats for the first time that night. The king of the pit in the centre, two veterans at his shoulders, the ogre half a step behind because otherwise he would have been too far forward. A dwarf with an orange crest and no shirt, laughing loudly and carrying no shield — the kind who has already thrown his life away and treats the remainder as a bonus, and who is therefore welcome in pits, because he never gives ground and the crowd mistakes that for courage. Then the light men, barefoot, javelins in the crook of an arm, spreading out to take the edges. And behind them the four with the brands. They did not fan out to take ground but to deny it: a half-circle with their own side at their backs, the light men and the branded in the front rank, the king, the veterans and the ogre behind them where nothing could tie them down and where they could take anyone who committed to the front from the flank a moment later. Then they waited. A house that fights in the same oval every night knows every foot of it, and knows that whoever runs first runs out of breath first.

All three came at them at once: the marauders from the left, the Children of Sotek up the middle, the caravan from the right. None of the three had exchanged a word with the others.

The half-circle held anyway, and the barefoot men were the reason. A saurus warrior who has got his weight moving cannot be stopped, but he can be steered, and that is precisely what they did: they waited until the lizard was on them, stepped aside at the last possible moment, and let him run through onto empty ground, where he then stood while three of their fellows re-formed at leisure. Twice, three times it went like that. The Children of Sotek understand a great many things, but they do not understand why a warrior would run away instead of fighting, and you could see on them exactly how little they understood it.

What the barefoot men failed to reckon with is that a trick which has to work every single time eventually does not. One of them put his foot down where the boy with the rake had not smoothed the sand; another misjudged a distance by a hand's breadth — and a man who once stands where a lizard is standing stays there for the rest of his evening. Only those with room can sidestep. The Children needed no second invitation; they piled onto anyone they finally got hold of, three and four at a time, and what they did then was no longer fighting but what you do to something that has made a fool of you for too long.

After that the half-circle did not hold. Sir Honnung, who had been fending off a wolf a moment before, saw a gap and drove into it, and after that there was no order left, only sand and dust and men who had lost track of which colours they were supposed to be hitting.

The Children worked their way along the pillar, behind the spine, which covered their flank: two of the light men, then the dwarf, who laughed to the last, then one of the branded. The caravan held its own piece of ground and took one of the four and a spearman. The price for it was paid by Sir Olvir von der Krunkmark. The man from Nordland had stayed on his feet in the Rich Quarter with two wolves on him, and the caravan had grown used to putting him wherever the ground was tightest. That night three of them got to him at once, two working their javelins at close quarters like short spears. What looked from the tiers like a stumble was something else entirely for his leg. The Norsemen came in from the right, and they did not come the way the tiers were used to.

A pit fight lives on two men circling until one of them makes the mistake. What Rangvald did there had nothing to do with that. He went into the open flank because it was open, and he went in a straight line, because that is the shortest way. Ulfrik the Impaler went on his left, Wulfrik Shiptaker on his right, and between the three of them there was no room a defender could have stepped into. The two veterans at the king's side had survived years in that pit. They were given no chance to show it. The first turned to close the guard and went down under Rangvald's axe before his arm was up; the second saw it, took the one correct step, and by taking it stood in front of Wulfrik, who took him without altering his step. It did not last as long as it takes to read. The tiers did not get louder at that point, they got quieter, and Wendel Krumm struck out a price two men had paid for.

The pack was further along by then. Dogs that meet a branded man do not fight him; two fasten on an arm and three on a leg, and once the man is down the fight is over before it started. That is how the house lost one of the Four and one of the barefoot ones, who was still slipping aside when there was no longer anybody there to slip aside from.

So the Norsemen cleared everything between themselves and the middle and were left at last standing in front of only two: the ogre and the king.

The king they did not get. A dog got him. What was left of the pack came in from behind and from two sides, and a man with two weapons and no shield can defend against one of those. He was dragged down by the one the Reavers have called Sabrekiller since the Rich Quarter — not the same dog that brought the great cat to bay that night and died of the wound, but the one the pack passed the name on to, the way Norsemen pass names on: not to the eldest and not to the cleverest, but to the one who goes in first. He went in first, he went for the back of the knee, and by the time the king buckled the rest of the pack was already over him. What happened after that has nothing in common with single combat. A pack does not divide a man, it divides him up: one at the shoulder, two at the thigh, one at the throat, each pulling a different way, because not one of them knows it is working with the others. The king of the pit, who had never lost a fight in that place, made no sound that carried over the tiers. He was simply no longer there to see. The Sabrekiller did again what had earned him the title, and this time he survived it. The Pitmaster watched from the barrier as his finest asset disappeared under an animal not worth a hundred crowns, and did not move, because a pitmaster who climbs into the sand has already lost his business.

The ogre was still standing. Styrkarr Skysplitter, who had done nothing all evening but stand at the edge and count, started towards him.

### The Arc Rises

They had all fought past it. The spine lay around the foot of the pillar, a lazy arc of bone the Pitmaster left where it was because the crowd likes scenery and teeth sell one at a time. Two men had already ducked behind it that night to get their breath.

The arc stood up.

It did so without effort, like something that had never been lying down, only waiting. The chains that had hung from the rings for years with nothing on them rang once as the vertebrae passed, and then were still — whatever was rising had no further use for anything that had once been fastened to it. No flesh, no muscle doing the work; only, between the vertebrae, a light standing in the air like smoke refusing to rise, warm and slow and honey-coloured. The skull had no eyes. It still had plenty of teeth.

Up in the tiers, one man understood first what he was looking at, and screamed. Then all of them screamed. Three hundred people seated on slanted beams above a hole do not need long to turn a crowd into a disaster; they pressed towards the shaft, and the shaft was narrow, and what happened on that ladder has no place in this account.

The Pitmaster tried first what had always worked for him. He stepped to the barrier, raised both arms and shouted that it was part of the evening, that it had been paid for, that it was nothing but a piece of showmanship — and for a moment some of them even listened. Then the skull turned in his direction, and the man who owned the killing down there walked away from the edge like everybody else.

Wendel Krumm held out longest. The bookmaker stayed at his table, clutching the strongbox with the evening's stakes in it, and shouted for the men who were paid to protect him and the box. Not one of them came. In the end he was seen jamming the box under one arm and running with the rest, and what he could not carry he left on the board.

And the four doors stayed shut. The man with the barring beam had gone with the first of them, and nobody standing above that wall thought of going back for it. The wall looks different from below than it does from above: two men's height of stacked rubble levelled off at the top and crowned with sharpened stakes, set leaning inward so that nobody below gets the idea of taking hold of them, and over it a tier with three hundred people climbing across one another. The ladder was on the far side of that. It had never been meant for the ones in the sand.

When the tiers fell quiet, the only things left in the sand were three warbands, what remained of the house, and the arc.

Julius Broun stood twenty paces away and was the only one there with a name for it. He had had the name in his mouth since the descent, since the air had been too thick in that one place. He could have called it out. He could have told the others that nothing was standing up here that had ever died, but that something had moved into it which had never lived, and that you do not kill the one by breaking the other. Men in heavy coats had been sitting in the front row, and if twenty of them got up that ladder tonight, one of them would remember by morning who had named the winds aloud in the sand. Broun kept quiet, and went on keeping quiet even when somebody else said the thing he was not allowed to say. It is possible that his silence cost him more, later, than anything he took that night.

Yingling needed no time to understand and none to decide. What he did next, none of the men in the sand had expected.

The skink priest of the Children of Sotek began years ago to pick up the speech of men the way one picks up shells: singly, without system, and with nobody to correct him. He understands little of it and speaks less, and when he does it sounds like something arriving through water — too far back in the throat, without the endings men set such store by. He almost never does it. That night he turned to face the men, pointed at the arc of bone, and said, as loudly as he could:

"Not dead. Not animal. Wind in there."

Sir Honnung looked at him as though a wall had spoken. Marcus understood the words and not the sense. Robba, who trades from Marienburg to the borderlands with people whose languages she does not speak, understood both and took it for a priest's nonsense. The only man who knew that the lizard had just got it exactly right in five words stood twenty paces off and said nothing. The tongue of Sotek was not in chains. It was dead, and had been for some time, and something had settled into its shell and was wearing it like a stolen coat. For that, the order of the Old Ones no longer offers two answers, only one. The Children went in first.

It took one of them at once. The skull came down without the length behind it visibly moving at all, and a skink who had been running a moment before was not running any more. What followed, those standing close enough saw several times that night and never got used to: the light between the ribs thickened, as though someone had fed it, and the bones that had been splintered a moment earlier were not splintered any more.

At the far end of the oval the ogre was still on his feet. He had not seen what rose behind him, and it would not have concerned him if he had; an ogre in a pit works through whatever is in front of him.

What was in front of him was Styrkarr Skysplitter, and the seer's first act was what it always is: he saw to the pack. It is Styrkarr who handles the chieftain's dogs, not Rangvald, and he handles them with a plaited leather whip whose crack the animals have known since before their eyes opened. Where that crack lands — and it does not carry far, four paces at most — a dog goes one bite further than it would have gone on its own — not out of obedience, but because the pain across its back is worse than anything in front of it. That holds only while the seer himself is standing clear. A man with somebody at his throat has no hand for a whip and no breath for the crack.

Styrkarr sent the pack at the arc of bone, because that was where more teeth were needed. Then he turned towards the ogre, meaning to take him the way a Norseman takes such things.

He never arrived. The seer of the Reavers, whose word carries more weight in that warband than the chieftain's, took three steps, saw a mountain of meat turning slowly towards him, and stopped. Then he took a step back. Then another, and on the second he trod on the hem of his own cloak and spent a moment not knowing what to do with his feet — and anyone watching from the third row saw a small man in furs and feathers who could not cope with an ogre.

What came afterwards does not undo that, and Styrkarr knows it better than anyone. He raised his hand because there was nothing else left to do with it.

The Great Eagle throws nothing. He looks. What a seer does is draw his master's attention to one particular point in the sand, and what follows has nothing to do with the seer's strength and everything to do with the distance between two lives. The ogre had spent his hitting things for copper in a hole. Styrkarr had spent three winters at the edge of a world that refused to make up its mind, and had seen things along the way that cannot be unseen.

The tiers saw no lightning and no fire and heard nothing but the ordinary noise of three hundred people. They saw a very large man buckle in mid-turn, as though somebody had drawn the length out of his bones, and not get up again.

It could have gone the other way. Had the ogre had more behind him than the seer, the same distance would have come down on Styrkarr, and the chronicle of the Reavers would run differently from here. A seer of Tchar chooses with care where he directs his master's eye.

Then he turned round and saw where he had sent his dogs.

### The Fight for the Arc

Sir Honnung went in, and stood at the arc between two saurus warriors he had been fighting over an estate three weeks before. Walter, a sell-sword of the caravan who until then had done nothing that would have got him named by anybody, went with him.

And Magister Julius Broun went in third, with a dagger in each hand.

He insists on the title, even though nobody down there confers it and the people above who did confer it would rather see him burn today. What he can do is exactly one thing. He calls arrows out of nothing — silver, cold, half a dozen of them, circling him like wasps and waiting to be sent. They look like the end of a fight and they are not. The spell does not send them; the Magister has to do that himself, with the same hand and the same eye he would use on a crossbow, and Broun is a well-read man and a wretched shot. Of six arrows, one usually finds its mark, and what it does there amounts to roughly an ordinary bowshot. Against a man in leather that suffices on a good evening. Against a body of bone that missiles pass straight between the ribs of, it is nothing but a light show.

To which must be added a rule the Magister keeps because it is older than he is: a man with his arms full of arrows cannot call them while somebody is hanging at his throat. Into close quarters a wizard brings nothing but what he is already holding.

Broun was holding two daggers, and the second one is new. In the Rich Quarter he had knifed one of the Defilers, a man who had emptied temples and expected to die under an axe, and who was finished instead by somebody chiefly known until then for writing in the margins of books. Broun slept badly for two nights afterwards and on the third understood what he had actually discovered. The following week he bought the second dagger — not a sword, not a crossbow, but another of the same short ugly thing that had already saved his life once.

A scholar does not arm himself like a warrior. He arms himself like a man who wants to reproduce a result. What Broun has gained since is not courage but speed: the left hand comes along now without his having to send it. Beside him stood a chaos hound to whom it was evidently a matter of indifference that night who it was snarling at, and Broun has since conceded that he preferred the hound to the alternative.

It struck with the tail. Not aiming, but in a single long sweep that took half the sand, and whoever was not quick enough went down. A great many went down. Between the sweeps it breathed out, and what it breathed was not breath but the wind itself — a warm shove that drove dust and sand and men ahead of it and took their legs out from under them, and whoever got up had to make the distance all over again.

Two men stood all evening where marksmen have to stand if they do not want to hit their own from behind: Marcus at the caravan's edge, and across the sand, with the Children, the Great Crest Wonton, whose old wound from the quayside still shows on him, close beside Yingling, who was doing something other than shooting between shots. They shot into a press that moved faster than a marksman reloads and over which, at irregular intervals, something very long went sweeping. Neither of them saw the inside of a melee that night.

The Norsemen were late, and there were two reasons for it, only one of which is honourable. Their gate lay furthest of all from the pillar; they had the whole width of the sand in front of them, and in the middle of it stood the ogre, with no intention of falling. By the time Styrkarr put him down, the arc had already been over the Children twice.

The second reason is the one the saga of the Reavers will not mention.

Two of the Defilers went at it. These are men who strip the houses of gods and have never yet found anything inside one worth being afraid of. They got within five paces, and then came the thing everyone in that pit saw and the taverns of the East End were still repeating months later. One of them stopped as though he had walked into a wall nobody else could see. The other went backwards — not quickly, but in small, very careful steps, like a man crossing ice — holding his axe up in front of his face as if it had started to rain. Then the two of them stood there looking at each other, each hoping the other would move first, and neither did.

They would have preferred to tell it differently afterwards. Unfortunately there were still people in the tiers.

Two of the dogs fared the same way, and that was Styrkarr's own doing. He had sent them out ahead, far in front of his own line, and a dog that can no longer hear its handler's voice is not a war-beast but an animal. They came within a dozen paces, smelled something that smelled of nothing, and dropped flat into the sand, chins on their paws and ears back. One of them wet himself. There they stayed until it was over, and a spectator who had survived the night was loudly demanding his money back the following day.

In the end it was Wantan who brought it down. The second of the two Great Crests, whom nobody outside his own brood had ever taken any notice of, came through between two sweeps of the tail and struck where the vertebrae are thinnest — not with a leap, but by standing still until the arc came down again and then moving not one step too early. The skeleton buckled at the middle and came apart into itself, piece by piece, and the sand took it. It went quiet. Up in the tiers, where somebody was still left, one man clapped alone and then stopped.

Then the light began to beat again.

It lay between the ribs in the sand and grew brighter and dimmer, brighter and dimmer, in a rhythm everyone in that pit recognised, because each of them carried it in his own chest. The bones came back into the order they had just failed to hold, and the arc stood up, and it began again.

Twice it went that way. They put it down, and it took one of theirs and stood up whole.

It killed more slowly than it could have, and it killed steadily, and that is why nobody ran. A monster that rages drives men apart. One that works keeps them together. Somewhere between the first collapse and the second, the three warbands stopped noticing each other's colours. The Norsemen, arrived at last, struck from the same side as the Children of Sotek, and neither gave a step to keep clear of the other. Sir Honnung stood shoulder to shoulder with a saurus warrior for a full round and said afterwards only that the thing had smelled like a wet dog, and he did not mean the worm.

It was not an alliance. Nobody said a word about it, not that night and not since. It was what is left when an alliance becomes unnecessary.

The third time it was Broodfather Qotl, the same who had opened the evening in the bouts and taken the first wolf in mid-air. He came over the bones instead of round them, and what he did looked no different from the first time and the second. This time the light did not stay.

It did not go out. It rose. Between the ribs the honey-coloured thing came loose from the narrow place it had been standing in and drew upward in threads, slowly at first and then quickly, past the candles, through the smoke that had been standing under the ceiling for hours, and into the joints from which brick dust had been sifting all evening. Anyone still on his feet felt it across his face, warm and dry and smelling of nothing. Then it was gone, out into a city that lets no wind leave.

What remained was what had been lying there at the start: a spine as long as three men, curved in a lazy arc across the sand. The half-sold set of teeth the Pitmaster had not got round to lay scattered beside it.

And there was nobody else left.

The candles went on burning, because it had not occurred to anyone to put them out. Coats lay on the tiers, and jugs, and a single shoe. The stalls stood open, and so did the house's weapon racks, and on the bookmaker's table the ledger lay open at the odds for a bout nobody had settled. Three warbands who had given each other nothing since the quayside stood side by side for a long moment and looked at the same thing.

Then Yingling walked past Robba, stopped a pace in front of her, and looked at her the way lizards look: without blinking and without anything moving in the face. He pointed at the bones, then at her, then at himself, and said:

"Four times. You. Us. The others."

Robba asked what that was supposed to mean. The priest did not have the words for it, or had them and did not care to spend them. He went to the water behind the menagerie and stepped into it, and his people went after him.

Robba went first, and she did not go to the weapon racks. She went to the bookmaker's table.

The ledger was still there. The scale was there, the strongbox was gone, and the board beside it, where the pledges had lain all evening — the rings, the knife with the mother-of-pearl grip and a strap with a buckle of dull silver — was empty but for an outline in the dust.

A boy who had hidden behind the stalls and was therefore still alive told her that the little man had swept everything into the box with one movement of his forearm and then run.

It was a buckle. To Krumm it was what it weighed, and in his ledger it stood as a pledge against eighteen crowns. Robba could have paid that out of her purse without noticing it by the next morning. For four years she has converted everything that passed through her hands between Marienburg and the border into numbers, because that is her trade and because it works. It works on everything except three or four things, and whoever has the misfortune to own one of them will never find anyone to whom he can explain why he is standing there instead, looking at an outline in the dust.

She said nothing. She closed the ledger and took it with her, and it was only two days later, going through it out of sheer habit to see what the evening would have brought in, that she found the other list at the back: place, date, three blazons, four times beneath one another. At the quayside. At the library. In the Rich Quarter. And below that, in the same neat merchant's hand, the East End — written before the first of them had come down the ladder.

Robba asked Ottilie for her list that evening. The girl handed it over without saying anything, and the two lists agreed, except for the final entry, which only one of them had made in advance.

Where the man who writes the odds lives will be possible to find out.

Then they began to search, each warband for itself, and none of them stopped the others.

### Aftermath

For the Children of Sotek the pit was not a job. It was an answer.

Sotek is a serpent. What lay in the sand of that cavern was the spine of a serpent, put in irons, trained up for a farce, and risen three times after being struck down. A man calls that a coincidence because nothing better occurs to him. Yingling did not remark on it, but he went in first, and he was the only one who stood his ground rather than giving back when the bones gathered themselves the third time. Whatever he saw down there, it was not what the three hundred on the tiers were seeing. The next morning he drank from a well he would otherwise have left alone.

The Pitmaster paid out what he had promised the next morning, and paid it in full, which nobody had expected of him. He did not do it out of honesty. He did it because three hundred people had seen him walk away from the barrier, and because a man in his trade has nothing left but his word once his reputation is gone.

What the warbands carried out of the emptied pit he never asked back. It was the house's weapon racks that gave up most of it: eight daggers of the kind pressed into the hands of opening fighters, five of which went to the caravan, two to the Norsemen and one to the Children. Two swords out of the branded men's stock went to the Children as well, along with a jerkin of hardened leather with iron plates in it — a find nobody in a warband of lizards would have counted on, since what is worth a few crowns to a man has to be laboriously cut down and rebuilt before it will even sit on a skink, and then costs ten times as much. The Norsemen found the heavy gear: a full harness of rings and plates of the kind the king of the pit had worn, ownerless in one of the stalls.

The most valuable find was Rangvald's, and he did not make it among the weapons. In the room where the Pitmaster ran his business, between lists and promissory notes, lay a rolled map of the city. There are men in the villages around Mordheim who live by drawing such maps from memory, and most of them are inventions, or so old that the streets they show have been under rubble since the comet. Not this one. It was new, it was cleanly drawn, and anyone who holds it up against what is still standing finds the alleys exactly where they are marked. To Rangvald a map is not paper but an end to wandering: a man who knows where the houses of the rich stood, where the cellars ran deep enough, and which quarters the comet hit hardest, does not search any more. He goes there.

All three found wyrdstone, in the cracks between the tiers and in what the fleeing crowd had dropped, and nobody counted aloud.

In the days that followed, the Children searched the streets above the East End, and in the course of it they came to one of the public wells. Mordheim was proud of its water once. The wells stood roofed on pillars, carving around the rim and a fountain at the centre, and the burghers used to say that no city on the river drank better. Today every one of them holds the same thing: black water with everything that has washed into it since the comet settled at the bottom. That is exactly why warbands climb down into them. What lies down there is shards, and what comes back up is either a shard or a sickness.

It should have been Qotl. A saurus has meat and plate between himself and whatever he swallows, and Yingling knew that as well as anyone. He went down himself regardless, and nobody argued, because you do not argue with a priest who stood in front of his god's stolen coat that same week.

He came back up empty-handed. What he brought instead took hold on the third day: the same cough, the same white tongue, the same way of sitting quietly against a wall that had been seen in Sir Honnung after the house of books. The Children of Sotek will go into the next business without their priest, and Qotl will lead them.

What that means only becomes clear once a man has seen the two of them side by side. A saurus warrior comes out of the water for one purpose, and at that purpose he is complete: Qotl slips a blow without looking at it, he strikes the moment a guard drops, and in four battles he has not given a single step. But he does not decide where to go. He waits for somebody to show him the direction, the way those creatures rest on their feet and wait for a sound to set them moving. Yingling, meanwhile, small enough that a man could carry him, is the only one among them who can ask a question — and the only one to whom it occasionally occurs to ask one. He hears through water what is happening three streets away, he reads it, he chooses, and a single sword stroke would cut him in half.

Which is why neither of them has ever achieved anything alone. Together they are one warrior with a head. Apart, one is a head that cannot strike and the other an arm that does not know where.

What the caravan found was behind a locked door, and it was breathing. Under the stalls runs a row of cells in which the Pitmaster kept whatever he had not yet sent into the sand, and behind two of the doors sat no fighters but people in clothes that had once been good: a grain merchant from the outskirts, his sister, and two others who preferred not to say how they had got there. Robba did what a Marienburg merchant does. She did not offer them charity but a contract: escort as far as the gates, payable on arrival. The wagon took them out the next day, and what they paid came to more than the evening in the sand had earned.

One of them did not go. **Manfred**, he said his name was, and he said it the way a man says it who once had more to put after it — no house, no guild, no city. He had been in the cell for putting his fist into the face of one of the Pitmaster's men, which in the pit is not a crime but a debt, settled one way or another; he had settled his with four weeks under the sand. He wore a sword like a man who knows what it is for, and he still had it, because nobody had thought it worth taking off a man who was not coming back up anyway. He asked whether the caravan needed anybody. He asked once and waited for the answer without pressing, and that was the thing Robba noticed about him. A week earlier Robba would have had to wave him off; a train feeds as many mouths as its stores allow, and hers were spoken for. But on the way back she had bought a handwritten book from a trader out of the Little Moot, of the kind the halflings keep at home: recipes, passed along and copied out, that make a little into something that tastes like a great deal. In lean years a cook who can do that is worth more than another knight. Since the wagon started smelling the way it does in the evenings, people stop who would have walked on — and Manfred was the first of them.

The sell-swords took him in the way sell-swords do: on the second evening they tried calling him Wendel, then Wilhelm, because their names have all begun with the same letter since Marienburg and a new man puts up with it. He did not put up with it. He simply did not answer, for long enough that they gave it up, and he has stood at the wagon ever since as the only one whose name falls outside the pattern. Robba made a note of that without knowing what for.

Styrkarr attempted something that same night, and it failed. What he had seen in the sand would not let go of him: a body without flesh, moved by something that needs no body at all. A seer who sees that wants eyes afterwards that are not his own. The Norsemen gathered him what he asked for, and he burned it and scattered it and spoke it out as it must be spoken, and something came. It was a raven, as it usually is a raven. It sat for a while and looked at Styrkarr the way ravens look, and then flew off like a bird that is nothing more than a bird. What the seer spent on it is spent. He does not talk about it, and nobody asks him.

Two of the Children of Sotek did not go back into the water. A saurus warrior stayed in the sand where the tail had caught him, and with him the last skink of the brood that had run together since the quayside — first Quayside, then the library, then the estate, and now there was nobody left who could have told the story of that road. At the quay they had still carried their dead away. This time they left both of them lying where they lay, and none of the men dared watch them do it. It looked like indifference and was the opposite: you carry home what belongs to somebody, and of that brood there was nobody left for the two of them to belong to. Two more warriors and a skink carried their wounds home and will walk again.

Sir Olvir von der Krunkmark, who had stayed upright in the Rich Quarter under two wolves, went down this time. His leg did not hold under what went over him; the caravan's surgeon set it and then said what surgeons say in such cases, namely that it will knit if the man lets it. The knight from Nordland will be following the next business from the wagon, and everyone will hear him doing it.

Two of Rangvald's dogs came out of the sand the way dogs come out of sand: quietly, low to the ground, and hungry again by morning.

**Ottilie** did not shoot the wolf because she could. She did it because that night, for the second time, she understood that nobody is coming to do it for her — and because she had to walk three paces up to a live animal to be sure her one shot would not go into the sand. What stayed with her, though, was not the wolf but the light: the Pitmaster's lamps, with no oil burning in them but something in the oil, green and steady, and the sand beneath, where blood looked black. In a year on the road Robba taught her to judge the worth of goods by their weight. In one night under the East End, Ottilie learned to recognise the stone this whole city died for — and since then she finds it where others walk past broken glass.

**Marcus** did not lay a hand on an enemy. He stood at the edge, from the first call to the moment the light rose into the ceiling, and did nothing but load and shoot. A man doing that in a pit is not shooting at targets but into gaps that exist for half a heartbeat: between two men he must not hit, under a sweep that takes half the sand. The Tilean, who two battles ago was a nameless crossbowman, has stopped looking twice before he releases. In the pit that was the only way to hit anything at all. Outside it will save his life one day.

**Styrkarr Skysplitter** learned two things that night and repeats only one of them. The first is that his accusation drops an ogre without his ever touching it. The second is that he backed away from that same ogre and trod on his own cloak doing it, in front of three hundred strangers and his own warband — the man whose word weighs more here than the chieftain's, the man they follow because he hears what they cannot. A seer who discovers that his legs are not his own has two options afterwards. He can try to become a warrior, or he can stop pretending and go deeper into the thing that carries him anyway. Styrkarr chose the second. He calls differently since that night — shorter, surer, without the detours a man takes while he does not yet entirely believe his own god.

**Rangvald** missed something that night, and cannot say whether that was luck. The Norse do not pray to one god but to all of them: to the Four, to the ancestors, to the spirits of the fjords and the whales. Where a Kurgan knows his master by name, a Norseman calls into a crowd and never quite knows who answers. That is why the gods take longer over a Norseman. They look at him, but they look at him together, and what two of them would give him the other two withhold.

The night after the pit the chieftain sat at the fire and Styrkarr read what there was to read. The seer took a long time over it. In the end he said it had fallen short, and only just — as short as a man falls who gets his fingertips to a door frame and no further. What lay on the other side of that door he did not say, and he had good reason not to: whoever the gaze of the Four falls on is either marked by them or unmade by them, and which of the two it turns out to be is not the man's to choose. Rangvald received nothing. He also became nothing. For a chieftain who did not kill the king of the pit himself but whose dog did, that is a sour result, and he has asked twice since when the next venture is due.

**Ulfrik the Impaler** began the evening worse than anyone. A barefoot man with a dagger and a battered helmet did nothing more than not be where Ulfrik's blade came down, twice, and the Impaler was in the sand before the crowd had finished shouting his name. The galling part was not the defeat. It was that the man had not even looked fast doing it. What Ulfrik has been picking up since is not something a Norseman likes to admit: you can also survive a blow by stepping half a pace to the side of it instead of taking it.

**Wulfrik Shiptaker** killed a man who had survived years in that pit, and nobody will make a song about it anyway. With him that is precisely the point. He knocked a wolf down without breaking stride; he took the veteran without altering his step; he stood in the line while it held, and he was still standing in it when it did not. A man who fights in front of a crowd fights differently from one who is only taking the next step, and the house found out which of the two kills. Rangvald has used his name since without needing a reason to, which among the Reavers means something.

**Wantan** was, until that evening, the other Great Crest — the one you mention once you have already mentioned Wonton. That is over. He was the first of anyone to bring down the thing three warbands were standing in front of, and he did it by waiting instead of leaping. The Children have no word for that which would survive translation into a human tongue, but they position him differently now.

**Broodfather Qotl** killed three times that night what did not want to die, and twice had to watch it stand up again and eat its fill of his own people. The third time he went over the bones instead of round them. He carries two things away from it: the way you slip a sweep that takes half the sand — and what a warrior gets from standing three times within reach of something that does not tire, and still standing there the third time.

It is worth keeping in mind where this saurus comes from. A totem warrior is not appointed; he becomes one after killing twenty enemies and then bringing down the beast of his totem alone. Qotl had all of that behind him before he ever set foot in this city. What he has gained since the quayside, in four nights, amounts to roughly what it took him years to accumulate in Lustria. The skinks do not discuss it. Yingling has looked at him twice since the pit the way one looks at something growing faster than the plan allowed for.

**Wonton** spent the whole night beside Yingling, the two of them shooting at the same targets as they have since the quayside: the priest, who between two shots raises his hand and does something else with it, and beside him the Great Crest, who keeps the gap open until the priest is ready again. In a press where every few moments somebody drops and reveals somebody who was not there before, the only man who achieves anything is the one who already has the second arrow on its way before the first arrives. Wonton learned it because he had no other option: with what the fog at the quayside left him, he no longer gets anywhere in time.

The thing only got a name later, and the name did not come from the warbands. In the taverns of the East End, where every disaster becomes a story inside a week, they spoke of the **Amber Worm** — after the light, not after the bones. Anyone who had actually been there said nothing at all, which only improved the story.

Styrkarr finished his reckoning once they were above ground again. Four meetings, and the fourth he had taken either for no meeting at all or for one that not everybody climbs back out of. Both came true and neither did: some stayed below, but they were not his. What occupies him now is a different number. Between the four and the nine lie five, and five is a long time when you know something is coming and not what.
