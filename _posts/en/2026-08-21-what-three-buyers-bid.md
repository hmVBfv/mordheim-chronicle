The granaries at the East End were once the pride of the city. Mordheim lived off the Stir, and everything the barges brought upriver out of Ostermark was stored here, in four brick warehouses whose cellars ran so deep that the grain stayed cool through high summer. When the comet fell the walls held at first. They gave way the week after, one by one, at night and without warning. What came down did not scatter — it folded inward. The vaults of all four cellars broke into one another and left a single space beneath the street, wide as a nave and tilted like a toppled jug. The beams of the upper floors wedged instead of snapping as they fell, and they have stood ever since in slanting tiers, one above the next, like the seating of a theatre nobody designed.

Somebody saw that for what it was. He does not give his name; they call him the Pitmaster and there it rests. What he does let people hear is his one rule: above ground the warbands may open each other's throats as they please, but down here the killing belongs to him, and he leases it out for a share. Anyone who comes down the shaft leaves his feuds at the foot of the ladder like a wet coat. Anyone who picks them up early is carried out by men who are paid to do it quietly.

You go down through the loading shaft the grain sacks used, a winch over a square hole in a yard where nothing else is left standing. The ladder is new, the wood still pale. It runs forty rungs into the dark, and halfway down the air changes. Above, Mordheim smells of wet soot and the sweetish nothing the city has been breathing out since the comet. Below, it smells of tallow, beer, unwashed men and, under all of it, unmistakably, of stable. And over everything lies the smell of old grain, seeping out of the walls and never quite gone — a warm, floury sweetness so friendly that in this room it becomes obscene.

It is bright down there, and that is the first thing that startles a newcomer. The Pitmaster is sparing about most things, but not about light: hundreds of tallow candles in iron rings along the beams, in rows that follow the crooked line of the collapse and therefore run straight nowhere. The smoke stands in bands beneath the ceiling and has nowhere to go, since the only way up is the shaft and the shaft is too narrow. It sits at a slant in the vault, which means there is exactly one spot on the sand from which a man can see a scrap of sky, and in it, on clear nights, the tips of two towers. It is the only part of the city that reaches down here. Fighters rarely stand there, and never for long. An hour down there and a man carries the smell of tallow in his hair for three days. Here and there among the candles hang other lamps, smaller, with thick glass, and the light that falls from them is not yellow but green: the Pitmaster has put wyrdstone in oil, because it burns for nothing and because it draws a crowd. Where that light touches the sand, blood looks black.

The sand is real and expensive. It comes up from the Stir in baskets, fresh every week, and lies in a shallow oval hollow where the floor of the deepest cellar gave out. Sand, because sand drinks: whatever falls on it is gone by morning, and a boy rakes the rest smooth. The boy costs nothing, because he is paid in food. The bowl lies deep. From the lowest row of seats down to the sand is the height of two men, and the wall between is whatever the collapse provided: wall stubs, beam ends, an overturned cart, wedged together and lashed with wire and topped with iron points that face inward. Nobody climbs that who is bleeding. Nobody climbs it who is not.

You get in only at sand level, through four doors set into that wall at even intervals — heavy plank gates faced with iron, fed from the old cellar passages behind them, barred from the outside by one man with a beam. That is the actual bargain you make coming down here: the way out is the ladder, and the ladder is not in the sand. The ladder is up with the spectators.

Behind it, rising up the slope of the fallen vault, sits the crowd. It is more mixed than you would expect in a dying city: sell-swords between contracts, junk dealers and fences out of the East End, a handful of men in good coats trying hard to look like tradesmen and failing, women who sell and women who bet and a few who do both. They sit on the slanted beams, many with their feet over nothing, and when the evening pleases them they stamp in time, and brick dust sifts down out of the ceiling joints onto the shoulders of everyone seated lower. Nobody looks up. They are used to it.

At the narrow end, where the vault lies deepest, the old sluice opens — the run that once washed the spoiled grain away. This is the menagerie. The Pitmaster keeps whatever he can get, and in these years Mordheim provides things it never used to: dogs too large to be dogs; something with too many legs under a tarpaulin, which is only ever seen at feeding; an ogre who sits in a corner rather than a cage, because he is paid to and because nobody cares to ask him to leave. It is wet there. The water stands ankle-deep in the run itself and creeps further down whenever it rains outside, which is why that side of the pit smells not of tallow but of river. None of it reaches the sand bowl. The Pitmaster had the floor there built up until it sat higher than the mouth of the sluice, because wet sand does not drink, and a bowl with blood standing in it cannot be raked.

In the sand itself, set a little off centre, stands a pillar — a remnant of the old vaulting that the collapse left upright, and which the Pitmaster has fitted with iron rings, because a pillar with rings holds a great many things that will not otherwise be held. It carries nothing any more; the ceiling above it has been holding itself up for thirty years. It simply stands there in the middle of the light, and everything that happens in this bowl happens around it. Two of the rings still carry their chains. And around the foot of the pillar lies the one thing he never had hauled away: a spine as long as three men, curved in a wide and lazy arc, half sunk into the dry sand. It belonged to the prize of the house, a serpent taken out of the flooded cellars, hooks through its jaw and trained to kill at the sound of a horn, tethered to those same rings. Why it was left lying he does not say; ask him and he will tell you the crowd likes bones in the sand, it saves on scenery. He sells the teeth one at a time and is already halfway through them. Anyone walking past with a lantern at night sees a light between the ribs that does not come from the lantern: warm, slow, honey-coloured, like amber lit from within. The lads who rake the sand have taken to going the long way round, and give no reason for it.

The fighters wait opposite, in four stalls under the barrier that used to be grain rooms and still carry the iron rings the scales hung from. They are not the rings the old fighters hung from — Mordheim's old pits lay under the houses of the rich in the west, where the nobility kept their duellists and laid wagers the city did not discuss. Those pits are under rubble now. But a few of the men who fought in them are alive, walked free the night the comet fell, and came back because there is nothing else they know how to do. One of them stands in the Pitmaster's sand tonight with the brand of a house on his shoulder that no longer exists. They are what actually carries this place. The Pitmaster knows it. That is why he pays them well, why he never strikes them, and why he does not sleep nearby.

On the front bench, directly above the wall, where a man can look down into the sand from his seat, stands the only proper table in the house: the bookmaker's stall. On the table: a ledger, a strongbox, a scale for coins nobody recognises. Beside it, on a board, the pledges — what people put up when the gold runs out. Rings. A knife with a mother-of-pearl grip. A buckle.

Three weeks lay between the night in the Rich Quarter and the night this account concerns. They were weeks none of the three warbands had chosen: a wagon with a cracked axle stands until it no longer stands, a leg knits or it does not, and the dead have to be replaced before anyone goes anywhere again. In that time, spring ended. The Stir still carried the mountain melt and ran brown and high between the broken quays; columns of midges stood over the split cellars of the city, and in the courtyards where nobody lives any more something white was flowering out of the cracks between the stones that had not grown there before the comet. It was turning warm. In Mordheim that means the smell comes back — out of the cellars, off the river, out of everything lying under the rubble that kept quiet all winter.

In weeks like that, people go underground. It is cool down there, it smells honestly of tallow at least, and that is where the money is.

Nobody learns about the pit from a crier. You learn about it the way you learn about anything that pays well and can cost you your head: in stages, for a fee, and always from somebody who profits by your knowing.

Roberta van Dorp heard it from a fence in the East End, whom she had gone to see only to ask whether anything of hers had been offered for sale in the days after the estate. The man said no, which was a lie, and pushed something better across the table instead: there was fighting in the collapsed granary cellars, and whoever did not care to fight could wager, and whoever cared for neither could at least sell — because where men wager they also drink, and get bandaged, and buy straps. Robba did the arithmetic on the spot and found that three nights in the pit would put the wagon back on its wheels. She had lost more than two sell-swords the night of the estate: when she came round her pockets were lighter, and what had been taken could not be replaced, because it had never been valuable, only hers. A merchant does not grieve over gold; she enters it in the ledger. She went down the alley the fence had described to her — past the burnt-out dyeworks, then between two houses leaning together like a pair of drunks, and out at last into the yard where nothing stood but a winch over a hole. She left the wagon at the edge of the quarter with two sell-swords and took what was still standing: Ottilie, Marcus, both knights, Julius Broun, and the handful of men still working for her since the estate.

Two men took the toll in the yard. A third wired every blade to its scabbard as its owner went down — not so that nobody fights below, but so that nobody fights below who has not been paid for. When your name is called, a boy at the edge of the sand cuts the wire, and everything after that happens on account. Broun carried only a dagger at his belt, and something else that cannot be wired to a scabbard. The fourth man looked at him for a long moment and then waved him through.

On the second night Robba walked past the bookmaker's stall, glanced at the board of pledges, and stopped. From then on it was no longer about takings.

The pit came to the Reavers of its own accord. A week after the estate a man in good boots appeared at the edge of their camp with four armed men behind him, raised his empty hands and said he was buying. Fighters for the evening, against a share. Prisoners too, and for Norsemen he paid double, because the crowd enjoys hearing them roar. Rangvald let him finish. Then he asked who else had already agreed, since a Norseman does not fight nobodies. The man, who was not stupid, only too confident, listed what he had, and because he thought the caravan his finest catch he spoke their names the way a trader lays goods on a table. At one of them Rangvald stopped him and made him say it twice: Julius Broun.

After that there was no more talk of shares. No Reaver objects to a wizard as such; they keep one of their own and they do as he tells them. It was the manner in which the man had finished one of theirs in the Rich Quarter. The marauders Rangvald calls his Defilers did not earn the name in battle but after it: they are the ones sent into the houses of foreign gods. Whatever stands in a temple of Sigmar is not standing when they leave, and whatever lay inside it is lying in the street. A man like that expects to die under another warrior's axe, and expects it firmly; that is the bargain he struck with his god.

This one had been knifed by a scholar in gloves, from two paces, the way you open a sack. The man had not even raised a hand. He had put the blade in and then looked more startled by it than anyone else present — and that is what has stuck in the Reavers' throats ever since. Not that their man died. That he died for nothing, at the hand of somebody who did not even know what he was doing.

Ulfrik the Impaler wanted the pit to see the difference. Rangvald wanted the scholar to understand it before it came to that, and was not certain the two were the same wish. Styrkarr Skysplitter said nothing to either; he looked at the man in the good boots the way you look at someone who has not come but been sent. The Reavers did not arrive by the fence's alley or through the yard with the winch, but by the freight shaft on the far side, where the pit brings in its livestock — and they came in the company of the man in the good boots, who walked ahead of them sweating more than the night warranted.

The Children of Sotek heard it from nobody. They were never above ground. What men take Mordheim to be is only the lid: beneath it lie the flooded cellars, the granary sluices, the cisterns and the sewers, all of them broken into one another since the comet and forming now a single, slowly breathing waterway. The Children have been moving along that road for weeks, and they knew the way under the East End before the Pitmaster lowered his first cartload of sand. Yingling needed no rumour. He needed only what came through the water: the stamping of many feet on stone, three times in thirteen nights, always at the same hour. And beneath it, for weeks, a second sound — something very long dragging over wet ground, broken by the ring of chains pulling taut, then dragging again, then ringing again, night after night, along the same short run.

Water carries sound a long way and alters it very little. Whatever moved down there was big, lay long against the floor, and never got anywhere, because it was tied.

To men it would have been an animal, an expensive one, the kind you chain and feed and show off on a good evening. To Yingling it was never an animal. It was a tongue of Sotek, laid in iron and trained to perform, and for that the faith of the Children knows two answers: free it, or release it. Which of the two was the right one would become clear when they stood in front of it. Broodfather Qotl, who had given almost no sign of anything since the estate, lifted his head for the first time when the ringing came through the water.

They did not come down the ladder. They came up out of the water behind the menagerie, where the old sluice lets out, and the first man to see them was a stable hand who was never able to tell anyone about it.

One man in that sand could have said something, had he been allowed to. Julius Broun stopped on the way to the stalls without meaning to, like a man who feels breath at his ear. The air in the pit was too thick, and it was not equally thick everywhere; it was thickest where the bones lay. He knew that from the books he ought to have burned. Broun said nothing. A man who names the winds aloud while witch hunters in the front row are betting on their own fighters is the prize exhibit himself by morning.

The one who spoke the pattern aloud before anyone else was neither priest nor prophet, but a small, damp-eyed man named Wendel Krumm, who wrote the odds. Krumm believed in nothing except numbers, and it was numbers that were troubling him. He kept a ledger of every fight reported to him, including the ones out in the ruins, because rumour moves wagers. At the quayside: three blazons. At the library: the same three. In the Rich Quarter, where the business had ostensibly been about wolves: the same three again. Mordheim is vast and empty and thick with band-marks, and in a city of a hundred packs the same three do not meet three times running in places that have nothing to do with one another. And now they were together a fourth time, in a place they had reached by three roads, none of which leads to either of the others.

Krumm showed his list to Robba, because he took her for the sensible one of the three. Robba looked at it for a while and said it was no marvel, only market: where three buyers bid for the same goods they are bound to cross paths, and the goods in question happen to be scattered across the whole city. Krumm nodded and went on writing. Ottilie, standing behind her mistress and saying nothing, had been keeping a list of her own since that night, with place and date, and had stopped believing in the market.

Yingling would have answered the same question differently, if anyone had been able to ask him. The Great Plan of the Old Ones knows no accidents, only knots. Three threads that keep crossing at the same point are not a misfortune but a seam — someone is holding them together. The Children of Sotek were not looking for wyrdstone. They were looking for the hand on the thread, and they took men and Chaos alike for tools that knew nothing of their own purpose.

Until that night, chance had set the stage every time: fog, fire, a contract about wolves. This time nobody set it. Three warbands came down the same shaft from three directions, of their own free will, each for a reason the other two knew nothing about — and stood, once again, three together in the same light.

Styrkarr had barely spoken all evening. He stood at the edge of the sand with his lips moving, and the one marauder close enough to notice said afterwards that the seer had been counting. The quayside had been the first. The library the second. The estate the third. Three is the number of the Changer, and three times three is his completion, and between the one and the other lie six meetings that have not yet happened. The four, however, does not belong to Tchar. The Four are all of them: the Changer, the Blood God, the Seducer, the Father of Plagues, and where all four are looking at once nothing happens, because each of them stands in the others' way. A fourth meeting is therefore no good news to a seer of Tchar. It is a night on which his master is not working alone.

He was not looking at the crowd while he did it. He was looking at the pillar at the narrow end and the arc of bone at its foot, where something dead stood honey-coloured between the ribs. To a seer of Tchar, flesh is only a loan, and a body worn by something else is no abomination but a proof. That he was wrong about this, only the wizard could have told him, and the wizard kept quiet.

When the crowd began to stamp and the brick dust came down out of the ceiling in fine threads, Styrkarr went to Rangvald and told him that this was either not a true meeting at all — or else not everything that had come down tonight would be going back up. Rangvald asked which of the two. The seer answered that the sand would decide, and walked out into it first.
