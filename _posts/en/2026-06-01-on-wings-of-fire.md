In the year 1999 of Sigmar's reckoning, a sign appeared in the night sky over the Old World: a comet with two tails, such as the prophecies had promised for five hundred years to herald the god's second coming. Night after night it grew, until its fire bathed the heavens above the free imperial city of Mordheim in a restless glow and banished darkness itself from the streets — by day the sun seemed but a dull, cold thing beside it. In that northernmost city of Ostermark, where the Sisters of Sigmar kept their watch upon a rock amid the River Stir, people streamed together in their tens of thousands, and beneath the blazing star they forgot all shame. They danced, drank and revelled in the streets and far out beyond the walls, without rest, without sleep, as though there were no tomorrow — and for most of them there would be none.

For in the first moment of the first day of the new millennium, the hammer fell. It was not Sigmar who came on wings of fire, but his judgement. With a roar ten thousand times louder than thunder, the comet struck. The ground seemed to leap up to meet the fire; towers toppled, walls burst asunder, whole streets were torn away like dry leaves in a storm. In Altdorf, many days' journey distant, the temples trembled and stones fell from their vaults; deep beneath the mountains, fortresses of the Dwarfs that had stood for generations cracked and gave way. When the light died, nothing remained of Mordheim but charred, smoking ruins.

For seven days and seven nights a shroud of smoke lay over the city, and from its heart rose a heat as from a glowing furnace of the netherworld. A mist enveloped everything, lit from within by an eerie green shimmer and so hot that none dared approach. Only when it grudgingly lifted, after many weeks, could the boldest see what the star had left behind: there, where the hammer had fallen, now gaped a pit whose edges still burned at a touch and whose walls were smooth and clear as glass, melted in the fire of Sigmar's wrath. And in that glass, trapped like insects in amber, the faces of the dead stared out from their glassy tomb — frozen for all time in that night of revelry, the horror still burned into their features.

As the ruins cooled, the first of the lost returned, and with them came the rumours. A stone had been found among the rubble, a dark, green-glimmering shard that could work wonders: the sick were healed, the blind made to see, from dead ground tall trees grew overnight, and the dead, so it was whispered, could be roused and made to speak. Most fateful of all were the tidings that spread like wildfire through the Empire — that an alchemist in the service of the Count of Reikland had turned base lead into pure gold from the stone. Wyrdstone they called it thereafter, and it became coveted beyond what its weight in gold could ever have been. Yet the stone was a double-edged gift: its raw power burned the flesh of those who bore it too long, warped mind and form, and even the very air of the city had turned to poison for any who lingered in it.

And so it was that through the gargoyle-guarded gates of Mordheim — the dead in their niches witness to every arrival — a steady stream of men poured in. Beyond the shattered walls, shabby camps of tents rose from the mud, some little more than nests of murderers, others, like Sigmarhaven, almost towns in their own right, with hired watchmen and marketplaces where weapon-sellers and pedlars hawked their shoddy wares at ruinous prices. From every corner of the Old World they came: mercenaries, cultists, outcasts, the faithful and the faithless, men and non-men alike — each in search of the green stone that could mean wealth, power or damnation. Some came out of greed. Some out of desperation. Some because in the comet's fall they saw a sign — the first breath of the End Times drawing near.

### The Three Warbands

**The Ardent Caravan** — A trading caravan under the leadership of the Marienburg merchant **Roberta van Dorp**, called Robba, out of the City of Gold, where merchants and guilds rule and goods from Ulthuan to Cathay choke the wharves. It was outfitted for the longest and most perilous of all journeys in the Old World: ever since the Tilean brothers Ricco and Robbio first opened the road eastward, merchants after them have dared the Silk Road as far as the Sentinels, where the way forks — the Spice Route south into the Land of a Thousand Gods, the Ivory Road east through the warring tribes of Hobgoblins and the terrors of the Ogre Kingdoms, on to the fabled realms of Ind and Cathay, whose names are served up in every tavern wrapped in smoke and legend alike. Nine of every ten caravans never reach their goal; their bones bleach in the passes, their names scatter on the wind. To make hers the one that returns, Robba had gathered about her an escort as motley as the Old World itself: landless knights as her vanguard, hired sell-swords, a Tilean crossbowman, and a magician who sheltered beneath her wagon-covers from the witch hunters' pyres. Yet when the first rumours of Mordheim reached her — of the comet's fall, of the green stone, of the riches waiting in the ruins — the signs shifted. A single journey to Cathay could make a merchant a prince. A sack of wyrdstone could do the same in a week. And the caravan was already near.

**Children of Sotek** — A band of Lizardmen out of the distant, steaming jungles of Lustria, borne across the ocean and up the river, so far from the warmth of their home that the cold air of Ostermark made their limbs sluggish whenever the sun hid its face. Once their kind, and their silent masters the Slann, served the divine Old Ones — and since the great departure of those ancient lords from the world, the Children of Sotek guard every rite, every sign, every woven thread in the Great Plan of their lost gods as best they can. The seeming calm of their hidden people has long been broken: ever since the Dry Skins began to plunder their temple-cities, they have known no mercy. What drove them so far from the shores of Lustria remains their own cold-blooded secret — yet in the ruins of Mordheim they keep watch over a hidden chamber whose contents no dry-skin may ever look upon. Their leader, the snake-tattooed Sotek priest **Yingling**, watches the plunderers from hiding and looses his warriors only when they stray too near the secret. For the Old Plans know no coincidence. And where a star falls from the sky, the servants of the Old Ones follow the sign.

**Rangvald's Reavers** — Norse out of the jagged, sea-lashed north beyond the Sea of Claws, where the tribes dwell in fjord-cut mountain lands haunted by mutant trolls, giants and nameless things from the deep. Fur-clad warriors and seafarers both, they build long dragon-prowed ships in which they harry the coasts of the south and dare voyages far to the west. They revere a whole host of gods, ancestors and spirits whose names the civilised world speaks with dread — and whose favour is won by one road only: blood, spilled again and again in proof of one's worth. Their seer **Styrkarr Skysplitter** stands closest to those powers; branded upon his brow is the Mark of Tchar, the Great Eagle, whom the Empire names, with dread, Tzeentch. Rangvald, ambitious as many a jarl before him, leads the band — and for him the comet's fall was more than merely another pretext for plunder. In it he saw the first flicker of the End Times, and he would be the one to recognise its coming first, to serve its coming most zealously. To Mordheim he came not merely for loot, but for followers, for power, for a name his gods would know.

Three such bands met one another, all unknowing, in the fog of Quayside.
